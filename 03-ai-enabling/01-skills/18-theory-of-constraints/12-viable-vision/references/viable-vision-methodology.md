# Viable Vision Methodology

The Viable Vision process is a complete loop, not a one-time project. Each pass through the loop removes one constraint and exposes the next. The loop runs until the company reaches the throughput target or decides the remaining constraints are structural.

## The Full Loop

```
Current State Assessment
         ↓
  Identify the Constraint
         ↓
  Design the Mafia Offer
         ↓
  Exploit the Constraint
         ↓
  Subordinate Everything Else
         ↓
   Elevate the Constraint
         ↓
  Measure the Improvement
         ↓
  Return to Start (new constraint)
```

---

## Phase 1: Current State Assessment

Before identifying constraints, establish a baseline using Throughput Accounting:

- **T (Throughput):** Revenue minus truly variable costs
- **I (Investment):** Money tied up in the system
- **OE (Operating Expense):** All fixed costs

Calculate:
- Net profit = T - OE
- ROI = (T - OE) / I
- Throughput per constraint unit

The Viable Vision target: increase throughput until the business runs at a margin profile equivalent to "revenue = current profit" — meaning OE becomes a small fraction of T.

**What to look for in current state:**
- Where are orders/customers/deals being lost?
- Where is lead time longest?
- Where do you consistently fall short of commitments?
- What do your best customers complain about most?

These are symptoms of the constraint.

---

## Phase 2: Identify the Constraint

The constraint is the single thing that limits system throughput most. There is always exactly one binding constraint at any time. The goal is to find it, not to optimize everything simultaneously.

**Four constraint types:**

| Type | Definition | How to Recognize |
|------|-----------|-----------------|
| **Market** | Not enough demand | Pipeline thin, sales capacity idle, win rates low |
| **Capacity** | Can't deliver what's demanded | Backlog growing, lead times long, customers waiting |
| **Policy** | A rule that prevents optimal flow | "We don't do it that way" — a rule invented to cope with an old limitation |
| **Knowledge** | Don't know how to do something | Consistent quality problems, new market not understood |

**The hidden constraint: Policy**

Most companies have more policy constraints than they realize. Rules like "we only take on clients over $X," "our standard contract is 12 months," "we require 50% upfront" were probably invented for good reasons — but those reasons may no longer apply. Auditing your policies for their original rationale often reveals the fastest constraints to remove.

---

## Phase 3: Design the Mafia Offer

Once the constraint is identified, design an offer that:
1. Eliminates the customer pain caused by that constraint (from the customer's perspective)
2. Transfers risk from the customer to you
3. Is deliverable at profit because your constraint removal makes it possible

See `references/mafia-offer-design.md` for detailed design guidance.

**Critical test:** Can you make this offer profitably TODAY? If not, do you have a credible path to being able to make it profitably after constraint removal?

If the answer to both is no, you haven't found the right offer yet.

---

## Phase 4: Exploit the Constraint

Exploitation means getting maximum output from the constraint without spending a dollar to expand it. This is almost always possible because constraints are almost never running at true maximum useful output.

**Exploitation tactics by constraint type:**

| Constraint Type | Exploitation Tactics |
|----------------|---------------------|
| Market | Re-target the offer at highest-T customers; improve conversion at existing lead sources |
| Capacity | Eliminate waste at the constraint; ensure constraint never waits for non-constraint; reduce variability feeding the constraint |
| Policy | Change the rule. That's it. |
| Knowledge | Train intensively; hire one expert; buy the knowledge; partner |

**The exploitation principle:** Don't try to fix everything. Fix the constraint. Everything else is subordination.

---

## Phase 5: Subordinate Everything Else

Subordination is the discipline that kills Viable Vision implementations. It requires every non-constraint resource to change its behavior to serve the constraint's needs — even if that means those resources appear underutilized.

**What subordination looks like:**

- Sales stops selling what it's easiest to sell and starts selling what goes through the constraint most efficiently
- Marketing focuses on leads most likely to need the Mafia Offer
- Operations protects constraint time at all cost — no constraint downtime for anything
- Finance accepts that non-constraint idle time is not waste

**What subordination does NOT look like:**

- "We'll do both" — adding the new approach while keeping the old
- Optimizing non-constraints because they look inefficient
- Treating all resources as equally important

Failure to subordinate is the #1 reason Viable Vision implementations stall.

---

## Phase 6: Elevate the Constraint

Elevation means investing to increase the constraint's capacity. Do this only after:
- Exploitation is complete (no more waste at the constraint)
- Subordination is working (non-constraints are feeding the constraint properly)
- The constraint is still binding

Elevation options:
- Add capacity (hire, buy equipment, outsource)
- Reduce constraint consumption per unit (process redesign, automation)
- Redesign the offer to use less constraint time per sale

**Warning:** If you elevate before exploiting, you spend money to fix a problem that was partially self-inflicted.

---

## Phase 7: Measure the Improvement

After each loop, measure:

| Metric | What It Tells You |
|--------|------------------|
| T (Throughput) | Did total money-generating capacity increase? |
| T per constraint unit | Did constraint efficiency improve? |
| Lead time | Did the constraint relief speed delivery? |
| Win rate | Did the Mafia Offer improve market response? |
| Net profit | Did T grow faster than OE? |

**The key test:** Is the new constraint in a different place? If so, subordination worked and the loop continues. If the constraint didn't move, the intervention didn't fully work — diagnose why before proceeding.

## Common Failure Modes

**1. Wrong constraint identified**
Symptom: intervention doesn't improve throughput. Action: go back to current state assessment with fresh eyes.

**2. Mafia Offer not credible**
Symptom: customers are interested but won't commit. Action: identify what risk they're still bearing and transfer it.

**3. Subordination not happening**
Symptom: throughput improves marginally even though constraint is being exploited. Action: audit what non-constraints are doing that's consuming constraint capacity.

**4. Multiple constraints elevated simultaneously**
Symptom: revenue grows but margin doesn't; chaos in operations. Action: enforce the discipline — one constraint at a time.

**5. Policy constraints not recognized**
Symptom: "we've always done it this way" blocks exploitation. Action: explicitly ask "what rule do we have that prevents us from getting more from this constraint?"

## The Four-Year Target: Why It Works

Goldratt's claim is that companies systematically leave 3-5x their current profit in revenue on the table due to unremoved constraints. The math:

- A company making $1M profit on $10M revenue has a 10% margin
- If the market constraint were removed (better offer) and the capacity constraint removed (better delivery), throughput could double with OE growing only 10-20%
- Year 1: +20% T, +5% OE → net profit +40%
- Year 2: +30% T, +8% OE → net profit +80%
- Year 3: +25% T, +10% OE → net profit +120%
- Year 4: net profit = original revenue target met

The compounding effect of removing constraints in sequence — where each removal enables the next — is what makes the four-year target achievable.
