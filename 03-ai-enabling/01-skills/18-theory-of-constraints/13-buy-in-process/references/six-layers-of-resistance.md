# Six Layers of Resistance

Goldratt's Six Layers of Resistance is a framework for understanding why people don't adopt changes — and what to do about it. Each layer represents a specific type of concern that blocks buy-in. The layers are sequential: you must resolve each one before the next becomes accessible.

The central insight: resistance is not irrational. Every layer is a rational concern that deserves a rational response. The failure mode is applying the wrong response to the wrong concern.

---

## Layer 1: Disagreement on the Problem

### Definition
The stakeholder does not believe that the situation being addressed is actually a problem. They may be happy with the status quo, believe current performance is acceptable, or simply not see the evidence you see.

### How to Recognize It
- "Things are working fine"
- "We've always done it this way and it works"
- "I don't see the issue"
- "We're meeting our targets"
- Blank stares when you describe the problem
- Active reframing: "That's not a problem, that's just how this industry works"

### What NOT to Do
- Present your solution (they cannot evaluate a fix to a problem they don't believe exists)
- Use more data (data won't help if they're disputing the interpretation, not the numbers)
- Invoke authority ("the CEO thinks it's a problem") — this generates compliance, not buy-in
- Express frustration ("I can't believe you don't see this") — shuts down dialogue

### What TO Do
Start from a shared foundation: observable effects that both parties agree are undesirable. Don't start with your diagnosis — start with undesirable effects you can both see.

Then work backward together. Ask: "What might be causing all of these effects?" Let them participate in discovering the root cause. A root cause they helped identify cannot be disputed the same way.

The goal is not to convince them — it's to discover together. Co-authorship of the diagnosis creates genuine agreement.

### TOC Tool
**Current Reality Tree (CRT)** — maps undesirable effects to root causes through explicit cause-effect relationships.

## Layer 2: Disagreement on Direction of Solution

### Definition
The stakeholder accepts that there is a problem but believes your proposed approach is the wrong one. They typically have a different solution in mind.

### How to Recognize It
- "That's not the right way to address this"
- "Have you considered a different approach?"
- "We tried something like this before"
- Active counter-proposals
- Agreement on the problem combined with rejection of your framing
- "There's a better way"

### What NOT to Do
- Force your solution through using positional authority
- Dismiss their alternative as uninformed
- Compete to "win" the argument
- Accept their counter-proposal without examination (both solutions may be wrong)

### What TO Do
Both of you are likely right about something. The conflict exists because you hold different assumptions about what the right approach requires. Surface those assumptions.

Map the conflict using an Evaporating Cloud: What does each party need? What prerequisite are they trying to satisfy with their approach? What assumption creates the apparent conflict between the two directions?

When the assumption is visible, it can be challenged. Often this produces a third option that both parties can genuinely endorse.

### TOC Tool
**Evaporating Cloud (EC) / Conflict Resolution Diagram** — maps the underlying needs and assumptions that generate conflict between two apparently incompatible positions.

## Layer 3: Disagreement That the Solution Solves the Problem

### Definition
The stakeholder accepts the problem exists and accepts your direction in principle but is unconvinced that your specific solution will actually produce the desired outcome. They see a gap in your logic.

### How to Recognize It
- "That won't fix it"
- "We've tried something like that before"
- "The problem is more complex than that"
- Requests for proof or precedent
- "Even if you did that, you'd still have [problem]"
- Skepticism about the causal chain

### What NOT to Do
- Repeat the argument more forcefully
- Add more features to the solution in response
- Appeal to industry benchmarks or case studies alone (doesn't address their specific concern)
- Dismiss their skepticism as unsophisticated

### What TO Do
Make your logic explicit. Spell out every step in the cause-effect chain from your solution to the desired outcome. Make each step a testable proposition.

Then invite them to point to the step where the chain breaks. This is not adversarial — it's collaborative validation. If they find a real break, your solution needs work. If the break is based on a false assumption, you can address it specifically.

### TOC Tool
**Future Reality Tree (FRT)** — maps the causal pathway from proposed injections (actions) to desired effects, making every assumption explicit.

## Layer 4: Solution Will Cause Negative Side Effects

### Definition
The stakeholder is now conditionally bought in — they believe the solution will address the original problem — but they're concerned it will create new problems. This is a sophisticated and often valid concern.

### How to Recognize It
- "Yes, but what about..."
- "That will cause [other problem]"
- "You'll fix this and create three more problems"
- "What about the impact on [department/process/customer]"
- Conditional agreement: "I'd support it if not for..."

### What NOT to Do
- Dismiss the concern: "That won't happen"
- Defer: "We'll deal with that later" (implies you don't know how)
- Minimize: "That's a small price to pay" (invalidates their concern)
- Ignore it and proceed (the concern will become a self-fulfilling prophecy)

### What TO Do
Take the concern seriously. Extend your Future Reality Tree to explicitly model the negative branch they're worried about. Show either:
1. Why the negative effect won't follow from your solution (with explicit cause-effect reasoning)
2. What injection (supplementary action) will neutralize the negative effect

If the negative effect is real and you can't neutralize it, you need to redesign the solution. A solution with unacceptable side effects is not ready.

### TOC Tool
**Negative Branch (extension of FRT)** — models the undesirable effects that might follow from a proposed solution, and designs injections to neutralize them.

## Layer 5: Obstacles to Implementation

### Definition
The stakeholder is now fully bought in at the idea level — they agree it's the right thing to do and believe it will work — but they see practical obstacles that prevent implementation.

This is actually positive. Reaching Layer 5 means you have ideological agreement. Now the work is practical problem-solving.

### How to Recognize It
- "We'd need to..."
- "The problem is we don't have the budget/resources/authority"
- "That would require approval from..."
- Planning language mixed with obstacle language
- "In theory yes, but in practice..."
- "We'd have to first..."

### What NOT to Do
- Dismiss obstacles as excuses
- Override them with authority ("Just make it happen")
- Ignore them and force a timeline
- Treat obstacles as evidence they're not really bought in

### What TO Do
Map the path. Use a Prerequisite Tree to convert each "we can't because X" into "to achieve this, we first need to accomplish Y." Identify the intermediate objectives that will overcome each obstacle. This turns an obstacle list into a project plan.

The critical move: treat obstacles as navigation, not rejection.

### TOC Tool
**Prerequisite Tree (PRT)** — maps the obstacles between current state and goal, and derives the intermediate objectives needed to overcome each one.

## Layer 6: Unverbalized Fear

### Definition
The stakeholder has a concern they haven't articulated — and may not be consciously aware of. The fear is often personal rather than logical: fear of redundancy, fear of looking bad, fear of blame if the change fails, distrust of the person proposing the change, or trauma from previous failed change efforts.

This is the most important layer and the most commonly missed.

### How to Recognize It
- Unexplained pushback that doesn't fit any logical layer
- Agreement in meetings, inaction afterward
- "I'll think about it" — indefinitely
- Vague concerns that don't resolve when addressed
- Enthusiasm that evaporates between meetings
- Silence when you'd expect questions
- Disproportionate resistance relative to the apparent stakes

### What NOT to Do
- Push harder (increases the fear)
- Assume agreement because objections have stopped surfacing
- Present more data (this is not a data problem)
- Proceed over the person (they will find ways to derail that are hard to attribute to them)

### What TO Do
Create psychological safety for the real concern to emerge. This requires:
- Private conversation, not group settings
- Questions that open rather than close: "What am I missing?" "What would need to be true for this to feel safe to support?"
- Genuine listening without defending or persuading
- Acknowledgment of the human dimension: "I understand this kind of change can feel uncertain. What's your biggest concern?"

Sometimes the fear names itself. Sometimes you need to name it for them, gently: "Are you concerned about how this might affect your team's role?"

### TOC Tool
**Open conversation + psychological safety.** No formal diagram tool is appropriate at Layer 6. The tool is human dialogue.

## Using the Layers in Practice

**Diagnose before responding.** The most common mistake is jumping to Layer 2 responses when the person is at Layer 1. Listen for the characteristic language before choosing your response.

**The layers are sequential but not always linear.** People can cycle back to earlier layers as new information arrives or circumstances change. A person who resolved Layer 1 last month may return to it today if something undermined their confidence.

**One conversation, one layer.** Trying to address multiple layers in one conversation usually fails. Address the presenting layer, confirm resolution, then move on.

**Confirm before advancing.** Ask before moving forward: "Does that address your concern?" Don't assume yes. Silence is not yes.

**Layer 6 can appear at any time.** Unverbalized fear doesn't wait for Layers 1-5 to be resolved. Watch for it throughout.
