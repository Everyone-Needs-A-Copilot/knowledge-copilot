# Buy-In Process

When people resist change, the instinct is to push harder — more data, more authority, more repetition. Goldratt's observation is that this fails because it addresses the wrong problem. Resistance isn't stubbornness. It's a logical response to a specific unresolved concern. The Six Layers of Resistance framework identifies exactly what concern is blocking buy-in and maps it to the right response.

The key insight: you cannot skip layers. A person at Layer 1 (doesn't agree there's a problem) cannot process a Layer 3 argument (your solution solves the problem). They'll appear to agree and then do nothing. Buy-in requires working through the layers in sequence.

## The Six Layers

### Layer 1: Disagreement on the Problem

**"I don't see a problem here. Things are working fine."**

The person doesn't agree that the situation you're trying to fix is actually broken. Your urgency seems manufactured to them. They're not being difficult — from their vantage point, they genuinely don't see what you see.

**Signs you're here:** Blank stares. "It's always been this way." "We've been successful so far." Active reframing of your data.

**What NOT to do:** Present your solution. They can't evaluate a solution to a problem they don't believe exists.

**What TO do:** Build a shared Current Reality Tree (CRT). Start from observable effects both parties agree are undesirable. Work backward to show they share a common root cause. Do not assert the cause — discover it together.

**TOC tool:** Current Reality Tree (CRT)

---

### Layer 2: Disagreement on Direction of Solution

**"Okay, there's a problem. But that's not the right way to fix it."**

The person accepts the problem exists but believes your proposed direction is wrong. This is often the most intellectually rich layer — they may have good reasons.

**Signs you're here:** "Have you considered...?" "That's not how we've handled this before." "There's a better approach." Active counter-proposals.

**What NOT to do:** Force your solution through. Dismiss their alternative.

**What TO do:** Use the Evaporating Cloud (EC) to surface the underlying conflict. What assumptions are creating the apparent conflict? Often both parties are right about something and the disagreement is based on an assumption neither has examined.

**TOC tool:** Evaporating Cloud (EC) / Conflict Resolution Diagram

---

### Layer 3: Disagreement That the Solution Solves the Problem

**"Even if we did that, it wouldn't fix the problem."**

The person accepts the problem and accepts your direction in principle but is unconvinced your specific solution will produce the desired outcome. This is a logic gap in your chain of causality.

**Signs you're here:** "That won't work because..." "We tried something like that." "The problem is more complex than that." Requests for proof or precedent.

**What NOT to do:** Repeat the argument louder. Add more features to the solution.

**What TO do:** Build a Future Reality Tree (FRT) that explicitly maps the cause-and-effect chain from your solution to the desired outcomes. Make every logical step visible and testable. Invite them to identify where the chain breaks.

**TOC tool:** Future Reality Tree (FRT)

---

### Layer 4: Solution Will Cause Negative Side Effects

**"But if we do that, it'll also cause [bad thing]."**

The person accepts that the solution will solve the original problem but is concerned it will create new problems. This is important — they may be right. Side effects are real and must be addressed, not dismissed.

**Signs you're here:** "What about...?" "Yes, but..." "We'll fix one problem and create three more." Specific concerns about downstream effects.

**What NOT to do:** Minimize the concern. "That won't happen." "We'll deal with it later."

**What TO do:** Extend the Future Reality Tree to model the negative branch. Show explicitly why the negative effect won't occur, or how you'll prevent it. If the negative effect is real, design an injection — a supplementary action that neutralizes the side effect without undermining the solution.

**TOC tool:** Negative Branch (extension of FRT), Injection design

---

### Layer 5: Obstacles to Implementation

**"We can't do that. We don't have the budget / resources / authority / time."**

The person is now bought in on the idea — they agree it's the right thing to do — but sees practical barriers. This is actually a positive layer to reach. Obstacle-focused resistance is much easier to solve than idea-level resistance.

**Signs you're here:** "We'd need to..." "The problem is we don't have..." "That would require approval from..." Planning language mixed with obstacle language.

**What NOT to do:** Dismiss obstacles as excuses. Override them with authority.

**What TO do:** Use the Prerequisite Tree (PRT) to map the path from here to the goal. Identify intermediate objectives that overcome each obstacle. Convert "we can't because X" into "to do this, first we need to accomplish Y."

**TOC tool:** Prerequisite Tree (PRT)

---

### Layer 6: Unverbalized Fear

**Silence. Vague agreement followed by inaction. "I'll think about it."**

The person has a concern they haven't articulated — and may not be able to. This is often the deepest and most important layer. The fear might be: "This will make me redundant." "I'll look bad if this succeeds." "I don't trust the person proposing this." "Last time we tried something like this, I was blamed when it failed."

**Signs you're here:** Unexplained pushback. Agreement in meetings, resistance in actions. "Sure, we can try that" followed by nothing. Vague concerns that don't resolve.

**What NOT to do:** Push harder. Assume agreement because they stopped objecting.

**What TO do:** Create a safe space for the real concern to surface. Ask open-ended questions: "What am I missing?" "What would need to be true for this to work?" "Is there something about how this would land that concerns you?" Listen without defending.

**TOC tool:** Open conversation, psychological safety, one-on-one dialogue

## Layer Summary Table

| Layer | The Real Objection | TOC Tool | Key Question to Ask |
|-------|-------------------|----------|---------------------|
| 1 | "There's no problem" | CRT | "What effects do we both see that we both agree are undesirable?" |
| 2 | "Wrong direction" | EC | "What is the assumption that's creating the conflict between our approaches?" |
| 3 | "Won't fix it" | FRT | "Where exactly does the cause-effect chain break in your view?" |
| 4 | "Will cause damage" | FRT (negative branch) | "Walk me through what you're worried will happen." |
| 5 | "Can't implement it" | PRT | "What would we need to have in place first for this to work?" |
| 6 | "Something else" | Conversation | "What am I missing that you're not saying?" |

## The Process

Working through the layers in sequence:

1. **Diagnose the layer** — don't assume you know. Listen for the characteristic signs.
2. **Confirm you're at the right layer** — ask a probing question before deploying a tool.
3. **Use the right tool** — deploying the wrong tool at the wrong layer wastes time and creates frustration.
4. **Confirm resolution before advancing** — buy-in at one layer doesn't mean the next layer is clear. Check.
5. **Never skip** — if someone at Layer 1 suddenly says "okay, your solution is wrong" (Layer 2), they've jumped — but they may not have truly resolved Layer 1. Verify.

## Common Mistakes

**Mistake: Treating all resistance as Layer 2**

Most change leaders present their case and immediately respond to pushback as "they don't agree with my approach." But the pushback might be Layer 1 (they don't see a problem), which requires a completely different response.

**Mistake: Using data to answer a fear**

Data doesn't resolve Layer 6. A person who fears they'll be displaced by a change won't be reassured by ROI projections. Name the human concern before presenting the business case.

**Mistake: Getting buy-in once and declaring victory**

Buy-in is not a one-time event. It degrades. People re-enter earlier layers when circumstances change, new information arrives, or implementation friction mounts. Treat buy-in as ongoing, not completed.

## Scripts

### `scripts/resistance_mapper.py`

Classify objections into layers and map to recommended actions.

```bash
python3 scripts/resistance_mapper.py --file input.json
```

Input: JSON with `proposal`, `stakeholders[]`, `objections[]` (each with `stakeholder`, `objection`, `layer` optional).

Output: objections classified by layer, stakeholder resistance profile, recommended TOC tool and approach per layer.

### `scripts/stakeholder_analysis.py`

Map stakeholders to quadrants (champion/supporter/blocker/bystander) and calculate net support.

```bash
python3 scripts/stakeholder_analysis.py --file input.json
```

Input: JSON with `stakeholders[]` (each with `name`, `role`, `power` 1-5, `support` -5 to +5).

Output: quadrant mapping, net support score weighted by power, priority actions.
